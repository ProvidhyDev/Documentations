Platform Overview

What Providhy ERP is

Providhy is a multi-tenant ERP for Nepali businesses. A single deployment serves thousands of tenants (companies), each with its own data, its own chart of accounts, its own fiscal year, its own subscription, and its own approval policies. The platform covers the full accounting cycle - sales, purchase, inventory, general ledger, fiscal year, banking - plus the SaaS plumbing that makes a multi-tenant product operable: tenant onboarding, subscription and package management, entitlement checks, notifications, bulk migration, and reporting.

The business context

A tenant (a company) buys a subscription to the product: a package that bundles modules (sales, purchase, accounting, ...), sold either directly by Providhy or through agents (resellers). The tenant is onboarded by the CMS/PAM operations platform, which registers it in TMS, assigns its package/contract, creates its IAM users and roles, and seeds its configuration (chart of account, fiscal year, approval flows) from platform defaults. After that, the tenant's staff log in to the ERP frontend (apps-frontend) and run their day-to-day business: raise quotations, sell, deliver, invoice, receive purchase orders, buy, stock goods, post vouchers, close the fiscal year, and watch reports.

Two channels sit on top of the core ERP:

  • the eSewa miniapp - a federated-identity surface embedded in the eSewa wallet app, through which customers and merchants interact with their ERP (credit, settlements, payment requests, party data) without ERP credentials; the public website (Website-Service) and CMS/PAM platform, which handle marketing content, leads, and platform administration.

Where the money and the books move

  • Sales-to-cash: quotation -> sales order -> delivery note -> sales invoice -> customer payment (recorded by the Sales payment endpoints; the eSewa provider path via Payment-Service is not used in ProvidhyERP) or cheque. Every state change emits a notification (events), an activity log (events), and a Kafka record event; approved invoices create accounting journals in the ledger (Accounting) via internal hooks.
  • Procure-to-pay: purchase requisition -> purchase order -> goods received -> purchase invoice -> supplier payment. Same event pattern.
  • Stock: ProductManagement owns the product master, batches, and the stock ledger; stock adjustments flow through the same approval pattern and post to the ledger.
  • Ledger: Accounting owns the chart of accounts and the voucher family (journal, payment, receipt, contra) plus banks and bill settlement. Vouchers are the only money truth; module documents reference journals rather than own them.
  • Fiscal year: Configuration is the single source for each tenant's fiscal year; closure locks transactions and carries balances forward (Accounting get-opening-info / transfer-closing, reporting opening-history).
  • Reporting: no report reads a module database. All module writes are mirrored to Kafka, consumed into reporting's own warehouse (transaction views + ledger), and served from there - including a read replica.
  • Entitlements: before any module action passes the IAM permission check, the service's auth backend verifies the tenant's subscription/contract through the Package Registry's check endpoints.

Authentication model (verified per service)

  • IAM-Backend is the identity provider: it holds users, groups, feature/action permissions, and issues JWTs. It is the only service with global DRF authentication (CustomJWTAuthentication, IAM/IAM/settings.py:134-142) and per-anonymous/user throttles.
  • Every other service authenticates per-view with its own backend (e.g. IAMAuthenticationBackendSales in Sales, IAMAuthenticationBackendPurchase in Purchase, IAMAuthenticationReportBackend in reporting, IAMAuthenticationConfigBackend in Configuration). These backends verify the JWT (typically against IAM-Backend's token-verify endpoint) and resolve the tenant + user + permissions for the request. In the ERP chain that means up to two synchronous round-trips to IAM-Backend on every request (token verify + permission fetch) with no caching - traced in the Architecture Deep-Dive.
  • HasPermission wraps the auth: each view declares required_feature_permission (choices.AllFeatures.*) and required_action_permission, switched per HTTP method in initial() - the same pattern across Sales, Purchase, Accounting and the party services.
  • Entitlement gate: before a feature permission passes, the service checks the tenant's subscription/contract through the Package Registry's internal check endpoints (check-package, check-module, check-contract, ...).
  • Other surfaces: cms-iam backends for the CMS/PAM platform, internal backends (token or shared-password) for service-to-service calls, FederatedIdentityAuthentication/MiniAppAuthentication for the eSewa miniapp, empty auth classes for public routes, BasicAuthentication for ops log viewers. See IAM for the token lifecycle and sequence diagram: authentication for the request path.

Deployment shape (verified)

  • Services are containerized Django apps (container hostnames appear in ALLOWED_HOSTS, e.g. Sales core/settings.py:28: sales, reporting Reporting/settings.py:36: reporting).
  • Shared infrastructure: one PostgreSQL server hosting one database per service, one Redis (broker for Celery), one Kafka cluster (kafka-crs:9092, SASL).
  • Reporting runs a primary-replica router (Reporting/Reporting/settings.py:121) against a separate replica database (settings.py:112) so reporting reads never contend with module writes.
  • Frontends (apps-frontend, iam-frontend, tenantPortal-frontend, website, miniapp) are separate apps that call the services over HTTP; they are not part of these 16 repos (they were scanned at their production tags for FE-usage numbers).
